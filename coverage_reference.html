Build the swim-lane logic one step at a time

This opening walkthrough focuses on a single visual idea: why different coverage pathways create different observation windows. The right-hand illustration stays fixed in one place and becomes more complete as you scroll.

01 / The question

How does diagnosed dementia risk differ between autistic and non-autistic adults?

Before choosing a dataset, comparator or outcome algorithm, we need to understand when people are visible in claims and how that visibility overlaps with later-life ADRD risk.

Start with the risk window, then ask which data sources actually observe it.

Jump to the proposed US population strategy ↗
02 / The problem

No single claims source follows every autistic adult the same way.

Medicare FFS, Medicaid and commercial claims each capture different populations and different spans of adulthood. A person can be fully visible in one source, partially visible in another, and entirely absent from a third.

The observability problem comes before the cohort-design problem.

03 / Pathway A

Pathway A: disability-related Medicare entry can create strong late-life observation.

An autistic adult may begin in Medicaid-supported services and later enter Medicare before age 65 through disability. That can create meaningful claims visibility during the period when dementia outcomes become more relevant.

This is an illustrative pathway, not a statement about typical ages or guaranteed entitlement.

Compare with the full five-lane chart ↗
04 / Pathway B

Pathway B: commercial coverage can observe earlier adulthood but miss later risk years.

A different adult may be visible mainly through employer-sponsored insurance, then lose that observation before or around the time dementia risk starts to rise. Medicare may only capture a later slice of that history.

A long commercial history does not automatically translate into late-life ADRD follow-up.

05 / Pathway C

Pathway C: Medicaid contributes a different but important window into care.

For some autistic adults, Medicaid offers a distinct view of care, support needs and eligibility contexts. But that view depends on state rules, managed-care arrangements and encounter completeness.

Medicaid may add representation, but it does not behave like a national linked life-course cohort.

US Population Strategy: three complementary analytic windows

The proposed US arm uses distinct administrative sources for different scientific contributions. These would be separate analyses; there is no assumed person-level linkage or continuous follow-up as beneficiaries move between payers. Each source requires empirical feasibility checks.

Possible primary older-adult analysis

Medicare FFS

Well-established claims approaches to ADRD ascertainment, with substantial older-adult event burden. Can also include beneficiaries originally entitled through disability before age 65.

Feasibility question

Can we create comparable autistic and non-autistic groups with sufficient observable history and validated outcome definitions?

Earlier-adulthood extension

MarketScan

Commercially insured adults, including many under 65, can contribute earlier observation windows. Enrollment may be interrupted and dementia events may be rare.

Feasibility question

How many ASD adults have eligible follow-up and incident ADRD events, including by age and treatment status?

Complementary population

Selected Medicaid states

Could improve representation of adults with intellectual disability or greater support needs. Coverage and coding quality vary across states and programs.

Feasibility question

Which states and years offer adequate ASD/ID ascertainment, eligibility information, and observable dementia outcomes?

These are distinct target populations. Comparing their results requires attention to entitlement, age, Medicaid/Medicare Advantage coverage, treatment availability, coding completeness and selection bias; numerical estimates are not automatically transportable.

Separate exploratory research · Not an NIH grant aim

Related GLP-1 and dementia questions

Separate research ideas have examined whether the observed association between GLP-1 receptor agonist initiation and lower incident dementia risk varies by clinical stage, measured metabolic changes, genetic susceptibility, or autism status. These ideas arose outside the funded NIH ASD/ADRD risk study and are provided for scientific context, not as another funded research program. EVOKE/EVOKE+ studied progression in symptomatic Alzheimer's disease rather than dementia prevention in cognitively unimpaired adults.

Question 1 · Exploratory
Clinical stage and mediation

Medicare active-comparator analysis of dementia incidence, cognitive-stage differences and sex; Leumit measurements of weight and HbA1c to investigate metabolic mediation.

Question 2 · Exploratory
Alzheimer's polygenic risk

All of Us whole-genome sequencing to evaluate whether AD genetic susceptibility modifies observed GLP-1/ADRD associations.

Question 3 · Exploratory
Autism-focused pharmacoepidemiology

Proposed MarketScan active-comparator, new-user analysis among autistic adults with type 2 diabetes, subject to data access, cohort feasibility and outcome events.

These are separate, exploratory research directions, not aims of the funded NIH ASD/ADRD grant or deliverables of a submitted or awarded fellowship. Observational associations do not establish treatment prevention effects. Any ASD/T2D GLP-1 analysis would require separate feasibility checks for ASD ascertainment, treatment eligibility, usable follow-up and incident ADRD events.

What is proposed, what needs data, and what remains open?

These items separate currently discussed directions from validated evidence and final protocol decisions. No selections below should be treated as investigator approval.

Proposed directions

  • Explore Medicare FFS as the primary older-adult US analysis.
  • Evaluate age-aligned, disability-entitled and dual-eligible comparisons in relation to the target estimand.
  • Explore MarketScan and selected Medicaid states as complementary cohorts.

Needs empirical assessment

  • ASD cohort and incident ADRD event counts by age, coverage and data source.
  • Attrition under alternative lookback, FFS eligibility and washout rules.
  • Medicaid state/year availability, coding completeness and ID classification.

Open methodological decisions

  • ASD diagnosis algorithm and ICD-9/ICD-10 cross-era handling.
  • Time zero, prevalence exclusion, gaps and mortality competing risks.
  • Bynum versus DREAM coding, care settings, spacing and sensitivity analyses.

Dynamic Cohort Design Rationale

Key decisions from the October 28, 2026 call, with supporting evidence from the literature.

No minimum enrollment requirement

ASD adults cycle between Medicaid and Medicare as their waiver and disability status changes. A 12-month continuous enrollment filter would exclude an estimated 50 to 60 percent of the ASD cohort (provisional estimate; to be confirmed in the study data). Under the dynamic open-cohort design, person-time accrues only in enrolled months; enrollment gaps suspend but do not terminate follow-up.

Open cohort with dynamic entry

Beneficiaries enter the ASD cohort when their second qualifying F84.x claim appears, following the Burke 2014 and Vivanti 2025 two-claim ascertainment approach.[1][2] A minimum 30-day separation between qualifying claims is a proposed operational requirement pending team confirmation of the specific interval. Entry is permitted in any calendar year from 2008 onward, not only at a fixed baseline date. Pre-October 2015 entry requires ICD-9-CM code 299.x; October 2015 onward uses ICD-10-CM F84.x. A stated rule is needed for linking qualifying claims across the coding transition (e.g., whether a 299.x claim and an F84.x claim within the same observation window count as the two-claim pair).

Dual enrollment and SES

Dual-enrolled ASD adults are not necessarily low-income. Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers carry higher or no income limits, so Medicaid eligibility via an HCBS developmental disability waiver does not imply poverty. The DUAL_ELGBL_MONS flag in MBSF identifies this population but does not bring Medicaid claims into the FFS environment.

Dementia ascertainment

Two validated claims-based approaches are available and closely related. Vivanti et al. 2025[2] used the Bynum-standard algorithm, described and validated by McCarthy et al. (2022) and Grodstein et al. (2022)[3][4]: qualifying dementia diagnosis codes in Medicare FFS Parts A/B claims only — either ≥1 inpatient/SNF/home health/hospice claim, or ≥2 carrier or outpatient claims at least 7 days apart. Part D medication claims are not included. Tobi's DREAM study (Desai et al. 2025)[5] uses diagnosis codes in 1 inpatient or 2 outpatient claims with no Part D component — structurally similar to Bynum; exact qualifying code lists and observation windows will need to be reconciled. The team protocol will specify which standard to apply; the choice should be aligned across the group, particularly with Tobi.

Comparison Group Options

Five candidate comparator populations scored using provisional, qualitative heuristics. The most appropriate comparator depends on the target estimand; scorecard ratings are not a validated ranking or a finalized protocol decision.

Socioeconomic Status Matching

SES is a major confounder in ADRD research: lower-SES individuals have higher comorbidity burden, worse care access, and different medication use patterns. Because ASD diagnosis and coverage pathways also differ substantially by SES (a late-diagnosed adult on commercial insurance until 65 looks very different from a Medicaid-enrolled adult since childhood), whichever comparison group is selected will require SES adjustment or matching. In Medicare and Medicaid claims data, SES is typically proxied by dual-enrollment status, area deprivation index (ADI) tied to zip code or census tract, or low-income subsidy (LIS) receipt under Part D. SES matchability is scored explicitly as one of the five dimensions on the comparison cards below. The score reflects how readily available SES proxies (dual-enrollment status, ADI, LIS receipt) can be used to match or adjust for SES differences between the ASD cohort and each candidate comparison group.

1Burke JP, Jain A, Yang W, Kelly JP, Kaiser M, Becker L, Lawer L, Newschaffer CJ. Does a claims diagnosis of autism mean a true case? Autism. 2014;18(3):321–330. doi:10.1177/1362361312467709
Establishes the PPV standard for 2+ ASD diagnosis code claims on separate dates (PPV 87.4% in a pediatric Medicaid validation sample). Supports the two-claim ascertainment approach; a 30-day minimum separation between claims is a proposed operational requirement — the specific interval should be confirmed against a published source before protocol finalization.
2Vivanti G, Lee W, Ventimiglia J, Tao S, Lyall K, Shea LL. Prevalence of Dementia Among US Adults With Autism Spectrum Disorder. JAMA Netw Open. 2025;8(1):e2453691. PMID: 39745705. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.53691
Analyzed 114,582 autistic adults aged 30+ using linked Medicare–Medicaid data (2014–2016). Three-year dementia prevalence: 8.03% (ASD without ID) and 8.88% (ASD with ID). Used the Bynum-standard dementia ascertainment algorithm. Did not include a non-ASD comparison group — cannot independently estimate ASD vs. non-ASD dementia risk. Provides large-scale ASD ascertainment and outcome precedent for Medicare–Medicaid dual-enrolled populations.

9Nikahd M, Hyer M, Wolf B, et al. Risk of Dementia and Related Neurocognitive Disorders Among Autistic and Non-Autistic Older Adults. Age Ageing. 2026;55(2):afag015. doi:10.1093/ageing/afag015

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Dementia
10Cummings JL, Atri A, Sano M, et al. EVOKE and EVOKE+: Oral semaglutide in early-stage symptomatic Alzheimer's disease (phase 3 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials). Lancet. 2026 Mar 19. doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(26)00459-9
Phase 3 RCT of oral semaglutide vs. placebo in amyloid-confirmed early-stage symptomatic AD. Did not slow cognitive decline on CDR-SB. Motivates the prevention-window hypothesis: GLP-1 benefit, if real, may require intervention before clinical memory symptoms are established.
11Tang H, Lu Y, Chen Y, et al. Mediation analysis of GLP-1 receptor agonist use and risk of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. Alzheimers Dement. 2025. doi:10.1002/alz.70161
T2D new-user cohort (n = 22,908; GLP-1 RA vs DPP-4i; Medicare FFS). Natural indirect effects through body weight and HbA1c ≈1.02 — metabolic pathways explain minimal share of the GLP-1/ADRD association. Relevant background for an exploratory metabolic mediation question; motivates further investigation of pathways not captured by measured changes.
12Wu C, Bhatt MM, Chen Y, et al. GLP-1 receptor agonist initiation and dementia risk: intention-to-treat versus on-treatment analysis. Published 2025.
Active comparator new-user cohort; GLP-1 RA vs DPP-4i. ITT HR 0.95; during-continuous-treatment HR 0.79. Treatment strategy materially changes the effect estimate. Pre-specification of the follow-up estimand is a key design decision for any related Medicare GLP-1 analysis.
13Zhou Y, et al. Comparative effectiveness of GLP-1 receptor agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors on dementia risk. Diabetes Obes Metab. 2026. doi:10.1111/dom.70384
Three-arm active comparator study. GLP-1 vs DPP-4i: HR 0.76; GLP-1 vs SGLT2i: HR 1.53. Comparator choice fundamentally changes the apparent treatment effect. Provides rationale for examining DPP-4i as an active comparator in a separate GLP-1/ADRD analysis; comparator choice still requires scientific justification.
14Schechter G, et al. GLP-1 receptor agonists and incident dementia in adults with type 2 diabetes and baseline mild cognitive impairment. Alzheimers Dement Transl Res Clin Interv. 2026. doi:10.1002/trc2.70315
Active comparator cohort in T2D + pre-existing MCI; GLP-1 RA vs DPP-4i. HR 0.74 (95% CI 0.57–0.95). Relevant to an exploratory question about baseline MCI and later dementia. However: this study examines MCI-to-dementia transition, not incident MCI in cognitively unimpaired adults — a distinct clinical question from the HR 0.983 in the James Medicare preliminary data.
15Siqueira JS, et al. GLP-1 receptor agonists and cognitive outcomes in Alzheimer's disease and mild cognitive impairment: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Published October 2026.
Meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (n = 4,132; symptomatic AD or MCI). Pooled CDR-SB effect: 0.09 (95% CI −0.10 to 0.28) — not statistically significant. Together with EVOKE [10], confirms null RCT evidence for treating established symptomatic disease and raises questions about whether observational associations differ by disease stage.
